## What kept tier 3 at 64 logical nodes from starting

Not the mesh. Every tier-3 node is a network namespace of one kernel, and a namespace's record
of its neighbours' Ethernet addresses is an entry in that kernel's one table: 64 nodes need 64 x
63 = 4,032 entries, past `net.ipv4.neigh.default.gc_thresh3`, 1,024 by default and in the
development VM, where 32 nodes' 992 are not. Past it the kernel cannot address a packet and drops
it - "neighbour: arp_cache: neighbor table overflow!", 250 lines in the VM's log in one start -
so calls time out ("the connection timed out", H24's stderr and again on `main` today), and the
lower nodes' calls, made one after another, hold every higher node behind them. With the table
raised for the run (`gc_thresh3` 32,768), `main` started 64 and 128 nodes over the same Pi-like
link; with it at 1,024 it started 32 and not 64, as H24 saw (`neighbour-table/`).

`tools/launch/tier3.sh` now refuses such a run by name before it makes a namespace, where the job
hung for 30 s and its nodes then declared each other vanished: "64 nodes need 4032 entries in the
kernel's neighbour table, which every namespace shares, and net.ipv4.neigh.default.gc_thresh3 is
1024: raise it to 4032 or more, and gc_thresh2 with it, or run fewer nodes" - checked in the VM,
and 32 nodes still started at the default. The limit is the host's, so the script leaves it
alone; H23's runs raised it and set it back. The README's neighbour-table sentence says that
logical nodes on one machine share the table, n x (n - 1) entries. H24's line in the record of
2026-10-13 put the failure down to the mesh; a note there now points here.

## The model

`tools/modelcheck/mesh_model.py`, registered as `model.mesh` (label `model`): every lead's calls
and accepts searched exhaustively, at two to five leads, in every order of the offsets, a lead
reaching the mesh at any moment - before it does, its kernel completes handshakes into its
listener's backlog, as the listener has been open since the lead advertised its port. A
connection is dialled into the backlog, answered (holding one of the listener's places, one in
the model where the C has 16), confirmed and attached by the caller, then attached by the callee.
Each lead runs one loop: it answers its listener, begins its next call when the call's slot is
due and fewer than W are in flight, and closes once its own calls and every caller the rule gives
it are in. Checked: no refusal (RULE), no wire attached twice (ONCE), no terminal state with a
lead still in its loop (STUCK), every pair's every wire at both ends (MESH). Each step moves a
connection or a lead forward, so the graph has no cycle, and a lead waiting for ever is exactly a
terminal state that is not the whole mesh.

The design holds at 2 to 5 leads, both wires a pair at two and three leads, one beyond, windows
of one, two and four calls, the slot clock where a call can wait past its slot - 3.5 million
states the largest case, two minutes and under 500 MB in all. Six leads are past an exhaustive
search (1.8 GB after nine minutes at a window of one, unfinished); five already have two offsets,
each a cycle through every lead. The order before H23 holds too (596,000 states at five leads):
its calls form no cycle. Each breakage fails its property, with a trace: calls made blocking, as
the old loop made them - the offset-1 calls are a cycle, every lead waiting on the next (STUCK, at
three and five leads); the hello's old check, "a lower node" (RULE: a call that wraps past the
last lead refused); both or neither of an opposite pair calling (ONCE, MESH); a listener closed
once a lead's own calls are done, callers still to come (STUCK); and a call dropped once its slot
has passed by a whole slot, as a loop that calls the current slot's offset would (STUCK).

Its `digest` writes the slots' orders - the offsets shuffled by Fisher-Yates with draws of
MurmurHash3's finalizer over the job, the step and the node count - for five jobs and 56 node
counts to 8,192 into `shmem/tests/mesh-schedule-digest.txt` (`model.mesh.digest` checks the model
still writes it, and `shmem.mesh-schedule` holds the C to it).

## The timing model

`tools/modelcheck/mesh_timing.py`, the proposal's sketch rewritten: every lead a serial server, a
connection a dial, the handshake, the hello, the answer, the confirmation and the two attaches,
over tier 3's `pi` link (100 µs each way, 1 Gbit/s), at the proposal's CPU of 35 µs at the caller
and 25 at the callee, two wires a pair; the leads entering at once, or each when the rendezvous's
tables reach it - a parent sending them to its children over one link, 156 bytes a node, so the
last level of 4,097 nodes arrives 0.16 s after the root. `check`, registered as
`model.mesh_timing`: each slot a permutation at every count to 129 and at 254, 255, 1,024 and
4,097, each lead its own order failing it (PERMUTATION); every caller a lead can have within the
listen backlog, `min(N, 4096)`, to 4,097 nodes (BACKLOG); the schedule at least four times the
old order at 64 and 256 nodes, both entries, the old order put through the same check failing it
(FASTER); and as the tables arrive at 256 nodes, at most 20 SYNs a round trip and 20 waiting
connections at a listener - shuffled 13 and 8, the offsets in increasing order 31 and 38
(SPREAD). Three seconds.

`table`, the slowest lead's last wire, a CPU a lead as on a cluster (`model/`):

| Nodes | Old order, at once | Schedule, at once | Old order, as the tables arrive | Schedule, as they arrive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 64 | 63.8 ms | 4.6 ms (13.7x) | 64.2 ms | 6.3 ms (10.2x) |
| 256 | 258 ms | 17.2 ms (15.0x) | 261 ms | 26.5 ms (9.8x) |
| 1,024 | 1,035 ms | 70.6 ms (14.7x) | 1,047 ms | 114 ms (9.2x) |
| 2,048 | 2,071 ms | 132 ms (15.7x) | 2,108 ms | 226 ms (9.3x) |
| 4,097 | 4,140 ms | 258 ms (16.0x) | 4,214 ms | 451 ms (9.3x) |

The schedule's SYNs at a listener within a round trip: 5 to 19, against the old order's 4 to 12;
its waiting connections at a lead 2 to 28 (the old order's 1 to 9), all inside the backlog. The
two largest counts took nine minutes on this Mac.

**The window.** The proposal's W was four calls when a pair was one connection; since the
control wire, four connections are two pairs. At 256 and 1,024 nodes, at once and as the tables
arrive, four pairs in flight (eight connections) are 1.4 to 1.7 times as fast as four
connections - 17 against 30 ms at 256 nodes at once - for a few more SYNs a round trip (9 to 19
against 7 to 14). The build takes eight, provisionally: below. **The shuffle** earns its place
where the leads arrive apart: at eight in flight, as the tables arrive at 256 nodes, 13 SYNs and
8 waiting at a listener against 31 and 38 with the offsets in increasing order, at 1,024 19 and
28 against 31 and 160; with every lead entering at once the increasing order is a little tidier
(5 SYNs against 9 at 256 nodes), and with one lead 20 ms late the two are alike (14 and 10 SYNs,
254 waiting at the late lead either way).

## The build

- **`mesh-schedule.[ch]`**: the rule (`osoe_mesh_schedule_calls`), whom a node calls at an
  offset, how many call it, the number of slots (n / 2) and the slots' order, line for line the
  model's `order`; `OSOE_MESH_CALLS_IN_FLIGHT` 8.
- **`listener.[ch]`**: the calling side one step at a time (`struct osoe_listener_call`: begin,
  poll, step, describe), a told port's and the port rule's candidates' ways both - dial within a
  second, the hello, the answer (a candidate's within a second, a told port's with no limit), the
  confirmation, each retry and back-off and every message as before. `osoe_listener_connect` and
  `osoe_listener_connect_port`, which the rendezvous and the tests below the library use, are that
  call and a loop that waits for it alone, their notices and fatals word for word. The accept
  loop's body is `osoe_listener_poll` and `osoe_listener_serve`, which a loop that waits on more
  can drive; `osoe_listener_accept_described` is the two in a loop.
- **`wire.c`'s `osoe_wires_open`**: one loop, its listener's places and socket and its calls in
  flight in one `ppoll`. Each pair's two calls in its slot; a call begun once its slot -
  `OSOE_MESH_SLOT` apart from the moment the rendezvous released the lead - has come and a place in
  the window is free, however late; the listener's callers judged by the schedule's rule ("node 2
  was called by node 0, which the mesh's schedule has node 2 call (§6.2)"), where the check was
  "not a lower node", and the "connected to twice" check kept. Its wait for a wire that does not
  come notices once 30 s have passed without one, naming in one line the nodes that have not
  called and the first call in flight - its node, host-list entry, address, port and last error -
  and how many of its own are left; the no-progress fatal says the same.
- **Settings**: `OSOE_MESH_SLOT`, microseconds, 0 to 100,000, default 100 (0: no slots), read at
  bootstrap and refused by name; and the test hook `OSOE_TEST_MESH_HOLD=NODE:MS`, a lead reaching
  its mesh late, refused by name without a node of the job or a time. Both in `--help`'s list,
  the known settings and the README's tables.
- **`SHMEM_DEBUG`**: each lead says how long its mesh took, from the rendezvous's release to its
  last wire, and its slots - the line that timed both orders below (the first commit of the build
  added it to the old order alone).

The wire's format is unchanged and so is the hello's version: the rule changes who calls, which
the decision of 2026-10-11 on mixed releases leaves as it is. The design notes' §6.2 carries "as
built since H23" notes, the proposal a status line, and the release notes a bullet.

## Tests and mutants

New: `shmem.mesh-schedule` (tier 1: one caller a pair, each slot a permutation, every node's calls
made, at every count to 300 and by the slots to 8,193, and 280 orders against the model's digest);
`shmem.mesh.refused-caller` (node 0 of three calling node 2, which node 2 calls itself - node 2
ends naming it); `shmem.mesh.hold` (seven nodes, node 3 held 1.5 s: its callers wait 1.51 s, its
own mesh 2 ms from its arrival), `.slot` (nine nodes at 20 ms slots: no lead under 60 ms), `.none`
(slots off) and `.crowd` (21 nodes at 1 µs slots, twelve of each lead's twenty calls waiting for a
place long after their slot); the refusals `shmem.start-up.mesh-slot{,-large}.invalid` and
`shmem.start-up.test-mesh-hold{,-node}.invalid`; and the models `model.mesh`, `model.mesh.digest`
and `model.mesh_timing`. `shmem.listener`'s stranger dials with its own blocking connect, the
helper it borrowed from `listener.c` gone.

Nine mutants, each failing its test and nothing else needed (`mutants/`): the draws without the
step, and the opposite pairs' upper node calling (`shmem.mesh-schedule`); the rule's check removed
(`.refused-caller`, node 2 then waiting for ever for its own call); `OSOE_MESH_SLOT` unbounded and
the hold's node unchecked (their refusals); the hold ignored (`.hold`); the slot clock ignored
(`.slot`); a call dropped once its slot has passed, the model's `skip_late` (`.crowd`); and the
listener left unanswered while a call of the lead's own is in flight, the model's `blocking`
(`.none`, eight nodes waiting on each other).

At 64 logical nodes over the Pi-like link the schedule connects the mesh in 82 to 105 ms where the
old order took 156 to 173 - 1.8 times, where a cluster would show the model's 10 to 14: every lead
here shares the VM's four CPUs, and every connection's CPU is the same in both orders, so the more

lead's work: within a sixth of both orders at 16 and 32 nodes, and short of both at 64 (96 ms for
the old order, 67 for the schedule), where 64 namespaces cost the VM more than it models. At 128
nodes some leads of either order finish about a second late - 36 of 128 in one run of the old
order, whose chain holds every lead behind a late one, 4 of 128 in one of the schedule's, none in
another - the mark of a packet's one-second retransmission in tier 3's 128 namespaces on one
bridge, not looked into (below).

## For the author

Each is the author's to decide; the build takes the first option of each until then.

1. **Connections on demand are a step of their own, not built.** The item's title lists them, the
   roadmap's recommendation for A8 that the author took named them ("build it now - the
   circulant model, tier 3's timing at 64 logical nodes, connections on demand"), and the
   proposal says to "do it together with on-demand connections (§6.2, 'The mesh is still
   eager'), which rewrite the same loop". The loop is the one piece they share. The design's
   own list of what on demand needs is the rest of the transport: the listener open for the
   job's life, served by a multishot accept on the ring; a PE that finds no connection toward a
   node asking its lead for one rather than reserving, since only the lead connects; a rule for
   two leads calling each other at once; the rings' bounds following the active peers - and
   beyond that list, everything that now assumes every wire exists from the start: the barrier's
   and the deadlock detector's tree on the control wires, the abort's goodbyes on every wire,
   keepalive and the silent-peer check, `osoe-stat`'s owners, the connection area's per-peer
   buffers. Each needs its model (a connection made while traffic waits for it; two leads
   crossing; an abort reaching a node no wire joins yet), and on demand changes what the README
   promises of start-up - a failure to reach a node met at its first put rather than in
   `shmem_init`. Options: (a) **an item of its own**, a proposal and models first, after 0.2 -
   recommended: the schedule has taken the start-up's cost at 4,097 nodes from 4.1 s to 0.26 s
   in the model, which was the eager mesh's cost the release meant to cut, and what on demand
   adds is fewer sockets for programs that talk to few nodes, a change of the start-up's
   promises that 0.2's tag need not carry; (b) an item of its own before 0.2's tag, since 0.2
   clears every open item; (c) dropped. Until the author decides, H23 stays open with this part
   alone.
2. **The window: eight connections in flight, four pairs** (`OSOE_MESH_CALLS_IN_FLIGHT`). The
   proposal's four was four pairs when a pair was one connection; four connections are two
   pairs. Options: (a) eight, as built - recommended: 1.4 to 1.7 times as fast on the Pi-like
   link in the model at 256 and 1,024 nodes, for 9 to 19 SYNs a round trip at a listener
   against 7 to 14, nothing a kernel's queue notices; (b) four, as the proposal's number reads.
   A constant, one line either way.
3. **Tier 3 past 32 nodes and the shared neighbour table.** `tier3.sh` refuses by name and
   leaves the host's limit alone, the run's harness raising it (as H23's did, and H24's harness,
   `collect-places.sh`, would have to at 64 and more). Options: (a) the refusal, as built -
   recommended: tier 3 keeps resolving addresses as a cluster does, and the script keeps its
   promise to change nothing outside its namespaces; (b) permanent neighbour entries made in
   every namespace, which the kernel leaves out of the count: any node count starts with the
   host untouched, but tier 3 no longer resolves an address, so ARP's part in a start goes
   untested there.
4. **A second's tail at 128 logical nodes on tier 3** (above): both orders, a few leads a
   second late in some runs. Likely a dropped packet's one-second retransmission - perhaps the
   ARP broadcasts of 128 namespaces on one bridge overrunning a queue - unconfirmed. Options:
   (a) an item to find it (`nstat`, `/proc/net/softnet_stat` around a run), small; (b) leave it,
   tier 3 at 128 nodes being past what the suite runs. Recommended (a), as a small item in the
   VM.
