### A2: the progress helpers' default, and a count rule

*What it is.* Each node's lead process has one progress thread, its agent, that sends and
receives every message between nodes. `OSOE_PROGRESS_HELPERS` adds threads that take over
the data wires, divided among them by peer node; the agent keeps the control wires (the
barrier's). The default is none until a fast link was measured (`docs/decisions/2026-10-07.md`,
items 4 to 6).

*The evidence* (H4: eight `c4-standard-16`, unbound, three rounds; the provider caps a node's
sending at 2,718 MiB/s, eight nodes at 21,744):

- **Streams**: no helper or one - the same, since one thread still holds every data wire - 67
  to 80% of the ceiling, less the more PEs a node; two helpers 97 to 99% at 8, 16 and 32 PEs a
  node; four, 1 to 2% more. Gets 31%, 35% and 64% faster at two helpers.
- **CPU**: the progress threads' seconds a GiB 30% more at two helpers, 70% more at four.
- **Collectives**: none slower; the alltoalls 15 to 27% faster at two helpers, 24 to 40% at
  four; a 64 KiB reduction 8 to 11% faster; the barrier unchanged.
- **The mailbox** (small messages with signals): 36% faster at two helpers with 8 PEs a node
  (one a core), 5% at 16 (one a vCPU), **55% slower at 32** (two a vCPU); four helpers already
  19% slower at 16.
- On the Pi's gigabit (round eight, S4): two helpers made a 64 KiB broadcast and a 4 KiB
  alltoall 6 to 35% slower under Slurm's binding, 2 to 9% unbound; the mailbox about a fifth
  faster.
- Not yet measured: the same under a site's binding, where a helper shares its PE's core unless
  the job reserves one (H26) - the case most sites run.

*Options.*
(a) None by default, as today; the README tells a fast link's users to set two.
(b) Two by default on a job of three nodes or more - two nodes have one peer, which one helper
already holds alone.
(c) As (b), but only where a node runs no more PEs than it has CPUs online (known at start-up,
before the count shapes the node's connection area), none otherwise: the oversubscribed
mailbox kept, the streams' gain there (46% at 32 PEs a node) given up.
(d) A rule from the measured link - helpers only where it outruns one thread: the link is
measured after the connection area is laid out, and its bandwidth read 1.9 to 5.1 GB/s from
job to job here (H14).

*Recommendation*: **(c)**, built in the VM now behind the decision and confirmed under a site's
binding (H26) at the third session before it becomes the default - every measured workload at
one PE a vCPU or fewer gains or holds, the Pi's losses at four PEs a node are small unbound,
and the one large loss, the mailbox oversubscribed, is the case it excludes.

### A3: the zero-copy send's default, and a third buffer

*What it is.* `OSOE_WIRE_ZERO_COPY=on` lends each send's buffer to the kernel
(`IORING_OP_SEND_ZC`) instead of copying it, until the kernel's notification; a connection
has two buffers, so one may be lent while the other copies. Off by default: over loopback it
lost 40%, and on the Pi it saved nothing.

*The evidence* (H8, 8 PEs a node, three rounds): with no helpers, where one thread holds the
rate back, it raised the stream 7 to 12% and took 6 to 9% off the progress CPU a GiB; with two
helpers, at the ceiling already, it changed neither rate nor CPU (1 to 3% more CPU). Every send
by reference had its notification, none was copied after all, and no case waited behind a
notification, so nothing asks for a third buffer. Locked memory is unlimited on these nodes; on
most systems it is 8 MiB, and a send the limit refuses goes again by copy, the connection
copying from then on, with a notice.

*Options.* (a) Off by default, two buffers, as today. (b) On by default for jobs of more than
one node. (c) On wherever helpers are off. And for the buffers: (i) two, as today; (ii) a third
while zero-copy is on.

*Recommendation*: **(a) and (i)**: it pays only where helpers do not run, which A2 is to make
rare on a fast link, it depends on a limit most systems keep low, and nothing showed a third
buffer would be used.

### A4: the ladder's defaults - the unsent limit and its rule, and the buffers

*What it is.* Each data wire limits the bytes queued in the kernel unsent, so that a small
operation is not stuck behind a stream: by default a rule from the link measured at start-up
(its bandwidth times a turnaround of 128 µs, a guess), or `OSOE_WIRE_UNSENT_BYTES`; beside it
the connection buffer (`OSOE_AM_BUFFER`, 256 KiB) and the receive buffers (`OSOE_WIRE_BYTES` ×
`OSOE_WIRE_BUFFERS`, 16 KiB × 64).

*The evidence.* The first session's ladder (H5): a fetch-add's round trip behind a stream at
the link's rate - 1,960 µs at the system's limit, 618 by the rule (about 417 KB there), 582 at
256 KiB and 524 at 64 KiB, the stream at the same rate in every case; a 128 KiB connection
buffer 516 µs, 1 MiB 747; 256 receive buffers 586, 64 KiB ones 567.
Tonight, at the image's settings, the rule's case again 605 µs (H27). And H14: the rule's input,
the bandwidth measured at start-up, read 1.9 to 5.1 GB/s from job to job, so the limit it sets
varied from about 220 to 530 KB between jobs. H6 - the ladder's code rungs, measured on the
trees before them - did not run; it would say what rungs already merged gave, and changes no
default.

*Options.* (a) Keep the rule and the buffers. (b) Halve the rule's turnaround, to about 200 KB
on this link, nearer the best measured. (c) A fixed limit, 256 KiB, in place of the rule.

*Recommendation*: **(a) for 0.2**: the rule takes two thirds off the system's delay at no cost
to the stream; a lower limit took about a sixth more off on one link, and the rule's input is
to be steadied (N2 below) before its target is moved.

### A6: the receive pool at many peers

*What it is.* Each owner of a node's wires receives into a pool of provided buffers (64 of 16
KiB); when the pool runs dry the kernel completes a receive with `-ENOBUFS` and the owner
re-arms it. The decision waited for H11, the cluster at size, which did not run.

*The evidence.* The first session's fan-in (seven nodes into one PE): 256 buffers cut the
re-arms about five times and raised no rate. Tonight's zero-copy runs, every node sending to
every other at 8 PEs a node: 12,000 to 26,000 re-arms in ten seconds on PE 0's node with no
helpers; with two, 1,000 to 2,000 in 1 MiB blocks and a few dozen in 64 KiB ones, the rate at
the ceiling.

*Options.* (a) Keep 64. (b) 256. (c) Wait for H11 at the third session.

*Recommendation*: **(a)**, now: eight nodes are this cluster's most, so H11 adds no peers to
what was measured, and more buffers have not raised a rate anywhere; a cluster of many more
nodes (the off-network one, N4) is where the pool should be measured again.

### Batch 1: P5 and P6

**P5, a team handle past 2^32 generations.** A created team's handle is its descriptor's
address plus its generation - how many times the PE has taken the number - times the size of
the PE's array of descriptors (design §5.7, D6), and the generation was 32 bits: the 2^32nd
take of one number ended the job, which a program splitting and destroying in a loop reaches
in hours. It is 64 bits now (`team.h`), private to the PE, bounded by what the sum can carry
below 2^64 - about 2^45 takes at the most numbers, computed when the array is made - with the
job's end named past that. A testing hook, `OSOE_TEST_TEAM_GENERATION`, starts every created
number at a generation (refused by name past the bound); `shmem.team-split.generations.L2`
takes one number eight times from 2^32 - 4, each team working and every handle another, and
the first, destroyed, still ends the job by name. With the field 32 bits again the job ends at
the fourth split and the test fails. Wrapping, the item's other way, would have given a
destroyed team's handle to a live one.

**P6, a split whose PEs pass different arguments.** §9.4.6 and §9.4.7 require every parent PE

arguments and the word's inverse beside the free masks and the marks (19 words of the two
lines' 32 at the most numbers): a bit some PE has and another has not is 0 in both ANDs, every
parent PE sees the same, and each ends the job by name with what it passed.

### T46: tier 3's one-second tail at 128 logical nodes

Measured by a Sonnet agent from the lead's brief (`docs/design/evidence/v0.2-step4/2026-10-20-t46/`,
its README the full account): 44 runs at 128 logical nodes on the Pi-like link, around each
every namespace's counters, the bridge's ports, the host's `softnet_stat` and `arp_cache`, and
in eight runs every node's ARP and TCP handshake frames captured with their times. At the
kernel's default backlog 12 of 30 runs had late leads - 45 of them, 410 to 968 ms behind the
run's median, with nothing between +190 and +410 ms. In the captured runs each of the 14 late
leads is one handshake frame lost - a SYN (4) or a SYN-ACK (13) sent and never received,
17 of the runs' 4,654 dropped frames - and the caller dialling again from a new port about a
second later (999 to 1,010 ms): osoe's dial deadline (`PROBE_NS`, 1 s, `listener.c`), which
fires just before TCP's own initial timeout would. The frames are dropped by the kernel's
per-CPU backlog (`net.core.netdev_max_backlog`, 1,000) on the bridge-to-node hop, which every
logical node shares - 128 nodes' ARP broadcasts arrive as 1.46 million frames a run for the
0.38 million sent. Raised to 65,536: no drop and no late lead in six runs of six, against four
of six at the default. ARP lost nothing that cost a second here, though a lost ARP frame can
(loss injected on two ports: 1.01 to 1.03 s in 3 of 39 pairs). Not shown: the drop site is
read from counters, not traced; what fills the queue beyond ARP's copies; and one run slow
throughout with no drop.

So the tail is the rehearsal's - one kernel's backlog serving 128 nodes - and not a defect of
osoe's, as the author's answer of 2026-10-19 expected. 65 items. Two choices it leaves, below.
